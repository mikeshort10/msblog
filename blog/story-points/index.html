Story Points

I've worked at a few different engineering shops using a variety of ticketing systems. Something that inevitably comes up when using these systems is "How do we want to track story points?". That leads me to respond with, "Well, what are they for?". Interestingly, the answer can vary wildly, from capacity planning to tracking "complexity" of tasks. In the article, I plan to discuss what I think story points should (and should not) be used for, what they should accomplish, how they should be tracked.

What are Story Points For?

I'd like to define some high level requirements for what a story point system should look like:

  1. It should be used to track time
  2. It should be simple to explain and remember
  3. You should be able to perform arithmetic on it

Some of these may seem ridiculous at first, but I'll got through each one and demonstrate how I've seen systems that have not abided by these and how that caused issues. Ultimately, we want to define a system that is both used and useful. If team members stop tracking story points or no one is using them, then we should just do away with them altogether.

Tracking Time

I've heard two main uses for story points: tracking time and tracking complexity. Which is more helpful? I'd start with asking what you would do with either. Tracking time makes sense. Managers can use them to determine who has capacity for unplanned work and can even push back on requests from the larger organization by saying that each member of the team is at capacity. Tracking complexity is less useful. What manager is going to push back on work because their team is dealing with a lot really complicated stuff right now? So if we use the system for tracking complexity, inevitably it will no be used, and so we should just do away with the system altogether.

I've also heard the argument that tracking time is nearly impossible. True, but I'd argue a similar difficulty with tracking complexity. At least time is more measurable than complexity. For instance, people use T-Shirt sizes to measure complexity, that is, small, medium, or large. How do you define a medium complexity task? You could have a list of criteria to measure against, but this would break requirement 4 above, since this list would need to be maintained, even iterated on, elsewhere. Time is much more straightforward. We can determine that we are splitting task into small (1 day), medium (2-3 days), and large (3+ days), and everyone knows what we mean instantly. Teammates still need to estimate the tasks appropriately, but at least we can agree one what the final verdict means and adjust our understanding together when we're wrong.

Simple to Explain

Writing up tickets in a ticket system or assigning points to existing tickets is no one favorite part of the job. Therefore, if we get value from the story pointing system, we should ensure that it's as simple to use as possible. If every teammate needs to reference a guide, especially one that may change over time, the points will mean very different things to different teammates, making the system useless. If the system is simple to understand, we can use it consistently.

Conforms to Arithmetic

This requirement sounds the most ridiculous, but it is extremely important. I've seen story point system that are either used as simply a ranking system, in some cases doing away with numbers altogether, e.g. T Shirt Sizes. What I think these systems are trying to accomplish is ease of use, since engineers only need to select from a prescribed list. However, this results in some odd math. For example, a real world system I've heard used is a Fibbonaci sequence for story points that works as follows:

  1. One day
  2. More than one day
  3. More than one week
  4. More than two weeks

In this system, 10 one point tickets are more points than 1 four point ticket. Maybe the intention isn't to do that, but then when we're trying to determine if the team or a particular teammate has time, how do we do that without looking at each ticket individually. If math can be performed on the tickets, we can impose guidelines during sprint planning, for instance in a normal two week sprint with each engineer having X hours of focus time, we need to ensure they have X +/- 10 hours of tickets.

What Meets These Requirements

The system is simple:

  1. Each point is equal to one hour of work
  2. Points should be a power of two, i.e. 1 hour, 2 hours, 4 hours, 8 hours etc.

These are the base requirements. I'd also recommend setting a cap, such as 32 points (4 full days), after which point the tickets need to be broke down more. This will depend on different workflows though. The system could still work with 256 point tickets, so we'll leave the limit off as a requirement.

This obviously meets the first requirement of representing time.

It also meets the second requirement, since 1 point == 1 hour. The second requirement can even be done away with. However, we think it actually adds to the simplicity of the system. By limiting the options, it becomes easier to choose. For instance, it's difficult to choose between assigning 4, 5, or 6 hours of work to something, but work is generally going to be more easily rounded to 4 or 8 hours. However, this is only a guideline. If you have work that definitely will take 5 hours, e.g. some kind of prescribed training, there's nothing stopping you from assigning 5 hours to the ticket. The defaults just make assigning a value easier when it's more of a guess. We think this is what the Fibonacci sequence systems try to accomplish, but it's easier to remember.

Finally, arithmetic can certainly be performed on it. For instance, if I have 1 four point ticket and you have 4 one point tickets, we should have roughly the same amount of estimated work. Capacity planning has just gotten easier.

What This System Is Not

It would be irresponsible to avoid talking about how this system can be abused and how story points have been abused. These are estimates. As a result, teammates should not be held responsible if the work takes longer than estimated. Estimating how long something will take before doing it is extremely hard, if not impossible. What should be done is a retrospective that evaluates why teammates made the wrong estimate, why points are carrying over to the next sprint. If we're blaming teammates for missing their mark, it will become impossible to get this closer to right over time. Honesty and psychological safety is the most important aspect of doing this.
